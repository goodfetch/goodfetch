# Sell operations, not browser-hours, from AWS

Goodfetch Cloud should make money the way every comparable cloud does, by selling operations around a free engine, but it should price each business in the unit its buyers already use, bill only verified success, and hold every Tenant (customer) to hard Limits (usage caps) drawn from one prepaid balance: **Compute Units (one gigabyte of memory for one hour) plus pay-per-event Recipes (packages of user code that creators publish) with a 20% marketplace commission, pages priced by class, active browser time plus metered traffic, proxy gigabytes and solves, and agent work at token cost plus a disclosed markup or at a fixed price per verified task**. It should launch entirely on AWS: managed Kubernetes (EKS) with the Karpenter autoscaler on AWS's own Graviton ARM processors, one engine per run, a Firecracker microVM (a tiny virtual machine that boots in a fraction of a second) for every untrusted Recipe and hosted browser on Graviton bare-metal servers, Spot capacity (spare machines sold at a deep discount that AWS can reclaim at two minutes' notice) for work that can checkpoint, Savings Plans (one- or three-year spend commitments that buy a discount) for live Sessions, no NAT Gateway (AWS's managed internet exit, which bills every gigabyte), and a Rust session gateway behind a Network Load Balancer, while keeping every fleet portable to cheaper bare metal. On that stack, the team's cost model puts a lean headless browser-hour at **$0.035**, a headful one with live view at **$0.14**, 1,000 static pages at **$0.003**, JavaScript pages at **$0.04**, protected pages at **$1.59** and a Compute Unit at **$0.01**, against volume market prices (list prices read on 12 October 2026) of $0.00–0.12 per browser-hour, about $0.22 and $0.60 per 1,000 static and JavaScript pages, $1.67 per 1,000 protected pages and $0.13 per Compute Unit. At market-median prices the four modelled revenue mixes earn a gross margin (revenue minus the direct cost of serving it) of **45–59% before overheads and 31–46% after them at $1M of monthly revenue**, and four levers that the engine and supplier contracts make available (metered direct traffic, a 15% token markup, 70% of protected pages served over HTTP, and residential bandwidth at $1/GB) lift the average from **39% to about 57%**. The bare browser-hour is the commodity: it earns 20% at the $0.08 median and loses money on headful live-view Sessions, so the money is in what rides on it. AWS stops being the cheapest home for steady browser capacity between roughly 1,000 and 10,000 concurrent browsers; at 10,000, a European bare-metal base cuts the fleet bill by a third to a half. Funnel scenarios, which are not forecasts, reach **$2.1M, $15M and $39M of annual recurring revenue by month 36**, the middle one about half of Apify's $31M today. The inputs that will decide the outcome are not AWS prices but measurements nobody has yet: the Goodfetch-built Chromium's real memory on Linux, fleet utilization, bytes per protected page, and how often pages need a browser or raise a challenge.

## Every comparable cloud opens the client, closes the fleet and isolates each Session

The first question has a short answer and a long tail. The open-source "cloud around a framework" layer that exists is mostly copyleft, the permissive pieces are components rather than a platform to fork, and the commercial clouds that matter have converged on one shape.

### No permissive multi-tenant platform exists to fork; the open parts are components

Where an open "platform around a framework" layer is substantive, it is copyleft or source-available, and it holds back the anti-bot engine that the vendor's cloud sells. Firecrawl's server is AGPL-3.0, and its self-hosting guide says its Fire-engine must be run "separately; it is not included", that screenshots and page actions need it, and that its Agent, Browser and interact products are cloud-only ([Firecrawl self-host docs](https://docs.firecrawl.dev/contributing/self-host)). Skyvern is AGPL-3.0 "with the exception of anti-bot measures available in our managed cloud" ([Skyvern README](https://github.com/Skyvern-AI/skyvern)). Lightpanda is AGPL-3.0-only ([Lightpanda LICENSING.md](https://github.com/lightpanda-io/browser/blob/HEAD/LICENSING.md)). Browserless is "SSPL-1.0 OR Browserless Commercial License" and requires the commercial license for closed-source commercial use ([Browserless LICENSE](https://github.com/browserless/browserless/blob/HEAD/LICENSE)), and Notte is SSPL ([Notte README](https://github.com/nottelabs/notte)). The Scrapy-era managers (Crawlab, Gerapy, ScrapydWeb, SpiderKeeper, Splash) are dormant, single-tenant admin panels; Crawlab's last stable release dates from 2023 ([Crawlab](https://github.com/crawlab-team/crawlab)). **No maintained, permissively licensed, multi-tenant actor platform with a marketplace exists in the open**, so Goodfetch cannot fork one. The best open specification of what such a platform does is Apify's Apache-2.0 Actor whitepaper, covering Docker-packaged programs, datasets, key-value stores, standby mode, multi-tenant standby and monetization ([Apify Actor whitepaper](https://github.com/apify/actor-whitepaper)); it reads as a checklist for the manifest and run model of Recipes, Goodfetch's portable packages of user code and the equivalent of Apify's Actors.

The permissive pieces are building blocks, and one is close to a reference design. E2B's runtime (Apache-2.0) is "the complete backend that powers E2B Cloud", and each of its sandboxes is a **Firecracker microVM**: a very small virtual machine with its own kernel, built by AWS for Lambda and Fargate, that boots a guest in at most 125 ms with at most 5 MiB of overhead ([Firecracker specification](https://github.com/firecracker-microvm/firecracker/blob/main/SPECIFICATION.md)). E2B never boots one from scratch: "A sandbox is a resumed snapshot", restored with memory pages loaded only when first touched (a Linux feature called userfaultfd), with writes going to a per-sandbox copy-on-write disk. Its control plane, which decides where a sandbox runs, and its data plane, which runs it, "never mix", and each sandbox gets "a per-sandbox nftables egress firewall and SNI/Host-inspecting domain allow and deny lists"; it ships as Terraform for GCP, AWS and Azure ([e2b-dev/runtime](https://github.com/e2b-dev/runtime)). Kernel publishes its browser images (Apache-2.0) and hypeman (MIT), a runtime that snapshots VMs to disk and resumes them in milliseconds ([kernel-images](https://github.com/kernel/kernel-images), [hypeman](https://github.com/kernel/hypeman)); Selenium Grid's chart scales browser nodes on pending requests with KEDA ([selenium-grid chart](https://github.com/SeleniumHQ/docker-selenium/blob/HEAD/charts/selenium-grid/README.md)); and Apify open-sources its proxy-chain, fingerprint-suite and impit libraries ([Apify on GitHub](https://github.com/apify)). For the cloud's own background work, Temporal and Hatchet are MIT, while Restate's BUSL bars "a Public Restate Platform Service" that lets third parties register their own deployments, which a design hosting Recipes as Restate services would trip ([Restate LICENSE](https://github.com/restatedev/restate/blob/HEAD/LICENSE)); Inngest is SSPL, Airbyte's platform ELv2, and OpenFaaS CE needs a commercial license ([OpenFaaS LICENSE](https://github.com/openfaas/faas/blob/HEAD/LICENSE)). Vendor-led open platforms drift: Daytona moved core development to a private codebase in June 2026 and archived its 71,565-star repository ([Daytona README](https://github.com/daytonaio/daytona)), and Trigger.dev keeps its checkpoints out of self-hosted builds ([Trigger.dev self-hosting](https://github.com/triggerdotdev/trigger.dev/blob/HEAD/docs/self-hosting/overview.mdx)). Critical paths are therefore safer on foundation-governed or widely forked projects such as Firecracker, gVisor, Kata, KEDA, Karpenter, Envoy, Temporal and Keycloak, and AGPL tools such as Lago belong only as unmodified, separate services.

The open servers also carry the field's clearest warnings. Scraping and browser servers become remote-code-execution surfaces, and targets of server-side request forgery (tricking a server into fetching internal addresses), as soon as they accept user-supplied browser flags, schemas, URLs or paths: Crawl4AI's Docker server had an unauthenticated remote code execution through Chromium launch arguments in June 2026 ([CVE-2026-57572](https://github.com/unclecode/crawl4ai/security/advisories/GHSA-r253-r9jw-qg44)), and Firecrawl one through its change-tracking git-diff mode in July ([CVE-2026-72905](https://github.com/firecrawl/firecrawl/security/advisories/GHSA-7843-ffpq-5f9p)). Young multi-tenant control planes ship cross-tenant bugs, such as Daytona's organization takeover ([CVE-2026-54320](https://github.com/daytonaio/daytona/security/advisories/GHSA-m6hx-cffh-3f3h)) and OpenMeter's critical SQL injection into shared ClickHouse meter queries ([CVE-2026-18801](https://github.com/openmeterio/openmeter/security/advisories/GHSA-m2fw-9wxq-jgf5)). Even VM boundaries broke: Kata had a guest-to-host escape through virtiofs ([CVE-2026-47243](https://github.com/kata-containers/kata-containers/security/advisories/GHSA-2gv2-cffp-j227)), Firecracker's jailer a host file overwrite ([CVE-2026-1386](https://github.com/firecracker-microvm/firecracker/security/advisories/GHSA-36j2-f825-qvgc)), and runc three container escapes in November 2025 ([oss-security](https://openwall.com/lists/oss-security/2025/11/05/3)). Each of these maps onto features that Foxhound and Collie, Goodfetch's crawl-and-extraction and automation capabilities, will expose through the crawl API and Recipes, which is why egress policy belongs at the VM boundary as well as in the engine, and why the deny list must include the ranges that defeated earlier fixes, such as 198.18.0.0/15 at Trigger.dev ([advisory](https://github.com/triggerdotdev/trigger.dev/security/advisories/GHSA-fhh4-xvj5-m7pq)) and carrier-grade NAT addresses at changedetection.io ([advisory](https://github.com/dgtlmoon/changedetection.io/security/advisories/GHSA-gwph-fp79-379w)). Gitpod's verdict after six years and about 1.5 million users on Kubernetes, the open-source system that schedules containers onto machines, that it "is not the right choice for building development environments" ([Ona](https://ona.com/stories/we-are-leaving-kubernetes)), applies just as well to long-lived, stateful browser Sessions run as plain pods.

### Comparable clouds rent AWS for control planes and cheaper hosts for heavy traffic

AWS runs most control planes, usually in one US Region. Apify runs on AWS us-east-1 across several Availability Zones ([Apify shared responsibility](https://docs.apify.com/security/shared-responsibility)); Browserbase offers four AWS-named Regions, and its load-balancer hostnames follow the pattern AWS generates for Kubernetes ingress ([Browserbase multi-region](https://docs.browserbase.com/guides/multi-region)); Browser Use runs Firecracker browsers on regular EC2 instances ([Browser Use Firecracker post](https://browser-use.com/posts/firecracker-browser-infra)). Firecrawl is the main exception, on Google Cloud, with Bigtable, Cloud Storage and Pub/Sub in its code ([bigtable-client.ts](https://github.com/firecrawl/firecrawl/blob/main/apps/api/src/lib/bigtable-client.ts)). Bandwidth-heavy layers go to cheaper hosts: Zyte lists Hetzner and Servers.com as hosting subprocessors beside AWS and Google Cloud ([Zyte subprocessors](https://www.zyte.com/terms-policies/subprocessor/)), and Kernel runs its browsers on bare metal ([Kernel: scaling Chromium on bare metal](https://www.kernel.sh/blog/scale)), most likely at Leaseweb, for which it maintains a CLI ([leaseweb-cli](https://github.com/kernel/leaseweb-cli)). Only hosted-browser vendors run several Regions, because each action a program sends to a remote browser over the Chrome DevTools Protocol (CDP), the connection used to drive Chrome, pays a network round trip: Browserbase cut network latency by 87–98% (Europe from 152 to 3 ms) by moving browsers near customers, and made multi-step tasks more than twice as fast ([Becoming multi-region](https://www.browserbase.com/blog/becoming-multi-region)).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Browserbase | AWS, four Regions, Kubernetes | dedicated VM per Session, destroyed afterwards ([security](https://docs.browserbase.com/account/enterprise/security)) | under 2 s ([blog](https://www.browserbase.com/blog/what-is-a-browserbase-browser)) | Stagehand (MIT) | fleet, CDP gateway, stealth, Functions |
| Browser Use | AWS; Firecracker on regular EC2 | Firecracker microVM per Session | 825 ms median ([post](https://browser-use.com/posts/firecracker-browser-infra)) | browser-use library (MIT) | Chromium fork, control plane |
| Kernel | API on AWS us-east-1 (by DNS); browsers on bare metal | unikernel or QEMU VM per browser | about 30 ms median ([docs](https://www.kernel.sh/docs)) | kernel-images (Apache-2.0), hypeman (MIT) | scheduler, standby proxy, stealth |
| Steel | AWS us-east-1 edge (by DNS), one Region today | VM per Session (Firecracker in 2024) | under 1 s ([steel.dev](https://steel.dev)) | steel-browser (Apache-2.0) | orchestrator, stealth, proxies |

Three engineering lessons recur across these clouds. First, **a VM per browser Session is the norm wherever untrusted parties drive browsers**; containers survive only where the operator controls the code (Zyte's stacks) or sells a cheap tier (Browserless's shared fleet). Second, **fast starts come from memory snapshots, not from idle browsers**: Kernel restores a Chromium VM from local disk in about 30 ms and moves browsers idle for 5 seconds into a snapshot "standby" that bills nothing while a proxy keeps the client's connection open ([Kernel standby](https://kernel.sh/docs/browsers/standby)), and Steel rejected Kubernetes warm pools because idle instances cost too much and booted too slowly ([Steel launch thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=42245573)). Third, **idle capacity is the dominant cost driver**: Browser Use says moving from Unikraft to Firecracker on regular EC2 cut its cost from $0.06 to $0.02 per browser-hour (a vendor claim), and it moved agent sandboxes to Bedrock AgentCore for having no idle bill ([AgentCore migration](https://browser-use.com/posts/agentcore-migration)); Firecrawl moved Redis off Railway after a roughly $15k bill that was mostly egress, then headed to Kubernetes because Fly.io would not autoscale smoothly ([An adventure in scaling](https://www.firecrawl.dev/blog/an-adventure-in-scaling)). The VM-isolated clouds run two orchestration layers, an ordinary Kubernetes or ECS layer for APIs and their own placement service for browsers (Browser Use, Kernel, Steel) or runs (Apify), and they build queues on databases they already run: Firecrawl's NuQ sits on Postgres with an 18-million-row production table ([nuq.sql](https://github.com/firecrawl/firecrawl/blob/main/apps/nuq-postgres/nuq.sql)), and Browser Use's agent runs sit on SQS with S3 checkpoints that re-enqueue themselves to get past Lambda's 15-minute limit ([production architecture](https://browser-use.com/posts/production-architecture-browser-use)). Their most common platform-wide failure is the control-plane database: Apify's primary cluster caused a full outage in February 2025 ([Apify postmortem](https://status.apify.com/incidents/4k6n5swkwx5h)) and a major one in July 2026 ([Apify incident](https://status.apify.com/incidents/fqkp6kdb0wy2)).

### Their business models sell operated capacity, and two financial profiles coexist

Nearly every company gives away an engine or SDK and sells operated capacity metered by usage, through self-serve plans topped by a sales-led enterprise tier that sells security and compliance: single sign-on, zero data retention, service-level agreements, health-data agreements and private networking ([Browserbase pricing](https://www.browserbase.com/pricing), [Firecrawl pricing](https://www.firecrawl.dev/pricing), [Kernel pricing](https://www.kernel.sh/pricing)). The on-ramp is always one API key and one switch to the cloud's base URL, such as the `APIFY_IS_AT_HOME` variable Apify injects into runs ([Apify environment variables](https://docs.apify.com/platform/actors/development/programming-interface/environment-variables)) or the `spider_cloud` feature of the MIT `spider` crate ([spider](https://github.com/spider-rs/spider)), while the fleet, the gateway, stealth, proxies, CAPTCHA solving and billing stay closed. Paid self-hosting licenses survive only where the engine is not permissive, as Browserless sells "Private deployments or licensed self-hosting" ([Browserless pricing](https://www.browserless.io/pricing)). Only Apify runs a marketplace at scale. The money splits into two profiles. Bootstrapped access and data businesses disclose real numbers: Apify reached about **$31M of annual recurring revenue (ARR) at the end of 2025 while profitable, with 27,000 paying customers among 56,000 active users** ([CzechCrunch](https://cc.cz/pred-lety-prosel-cesky-startup-odtucnovaci-kurou-ted-ho-o-to-vic-nakopl-boom-umele-inteligence/)); Bright Data passed $300M of annualized revenue growing over 50% ([Bright Data](https://brightdata.com/blog/general/data-for-ai-is-fueling-massive-growth-at-bright-data)); Oxylabs reported $350M of ARR at a $3.6B valuation ([Oxylabs](https://oxylabs.io/blog/oxylabs-receives-investment)), and its Lithuanian entity a 47.6% pre-tax margin in 2025 ([Signals Inbox](https://signalsinbox.com/blogs/news/ai-infrastructure-oxylabs-overvalued)). Agent-era API and browser companies raise money on adoption with little disclosed revenue: Browserbase at $300M post-money in June 2025 ([Browserbase](https://www.browserbase.com/blog/series-b-and-beyond)), Firecrawl with a $75M Series B in September 2026 ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/09/22/web-scraping-startup-firecrawl-closes-75m-investment/)), and Tavily, sold to Nebius for $275M ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-02-10/nebius-agrees-to-buy-ai-agent-search-company-tavily-for-275-million)) on about $2.8M of 2025 revenue by a Sacra-derived estimate. The useful benchmarks for Goodfetch are Apify, Bright Data and Oxylabs, not the venture valuations.

## Sell operations in each buyer's own unit, billed on verified success and capped

Most of the business-model question is already settled by a recorded decision, and the evidence supports it. Under `MIT OR Apache-2.0` anyone may self-host Goodfetch for free, so Goodfetch cannot sell a self-hosting license as Browserless and Lightpanda do; ADR 0018 instead has Goodfetch Cloud sell operations (fleet and isolation, managed proxies, hosted models, managed solving, multi-tenant Console screens, the marketplace and billing) and lets a capability leave the public framework only to become a Private component, a closed component licensed under contract to vetted customers, for legal exposure, never to sell it ([ADR 0018](../../../docs/adr/0018-permissive-license-private-components-only-for-legal-exposure.md)). That matches the closed set of every competitor above. The enterprise demand that Browserless and Lightpanda monetize as licenses must then be sold as operations too: a fleet dedicated to one customer or run inside the customer's own cloud account (bring your own cloud, or BYOC), SLAs, compliance documents, and Private components licensed to vetted customers, such as the Challenge solver's own logic, which ADR 0035 keeps closed (the Challenge solver is the component that answers CAPTCHAs and similar challenges) ([ADR 0035](../../../docs/adr/0035-stealth-is-a-settable-catalog-led-by-a-goodfetch-built-chromium.md)). BYOC is a differentiator rather than table stakes: Browserbase "doesn't offer on-prem deployment" ([Browserbase pricing](https://www.browserbase.com/pricing)), and only Anchor, Kadoa, Lightpanda and Browserless were found selling private deployments.

### One balance, four familiar units, and Limits before work starts

The pricing structure should follow what buyers rewarded and punished in 2025–2026. They punished opaque unit changes, such as Cursor's June 2025 switch to usage credits, which ended with an apology and refunds ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/07/07/cursor-apologizes-for-unclear-pricing-changes-that-upset-users/)); uncapped bills, such as Netlify's $104,500 bandwidth bill for a DDoS attack on a free site, forgiven only after it went viral ([Netlify forum](https://answers.netlify.com/t/netlify-billing-horror-story/113392)); and credit multipliers, expiring credits and billing for failures. Vendors converged on success-only billing (Zyte's customers are "only charged for successful Zyte API responses" ([Zyte docs](https://docs.zyte.com/zyte-api/pricing.html)); at Spider "Failed requests cost $0" ([Spider pricing](https://spider.cloud/pricing))), on credits that never expire (Browser Use, Spider), on prices known before the request (Parallel: "You always know the exact cost of a query before you run it" ([Parallel pricing](https://parallel.ai/pricing))) and on spend caps. Because the engine counts every primitive and knows no prices ([ADR 0001](../../../docs/adr/0001-single-tenant-engine-cloud-as-protocol-client.md)), one Usage ledger, the per-Tenant record of usage reserved and consumed, can price each business in its own buyers' unit instead of forcing one credit currency on all three. The structure I recommend is a single prepaid dollar balance that does not expire while the Tenant, the Goodfetch Cloud customer, is active, topped up by card, invoice or automatic recharge; each product billed in its own unit, and only on verified success; Limits, hard caps on usage, per run, per key and per Tenant, reserved before work starts, as Firecrawl's billing gate locks credits before a job runs ([firebill.ts](https://github.com/firecrawl/firecrawl/blob/main/apps/api/src/services/autumn/firebill.ts)) and as ADR 0041 already does locally ([ADR 0041](../../../docs/adr/0041-limits-across-runs-through-a-per-user-usage-ledger.md)); and a free allowance capped in currency, $5 a month as at Apify and Kernel ([Apify pricing](https://apify.com/pricing), [Kernel pricing](https://www.kernel.sh/pricing)), that excludes proxies and solving, because abuse is what free tiers really cost, as Heroku said when it ended its free plans ([Heroku](https://www.heroku.com/blog/next-chapter)).

| Business | Unit sold | Billing rule | Market anchor at volume | Where the margin comes from |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Recipe hosting and marketplace | Compute Unit (Apify's unit: 1 GB of memory held for one hour, with one CPU core per 4 GB) plus each Recipe's own per-event price | creators keep 80% of event revenue, net of the usage their paying users consume; Goodfetch absorbs free users' usage | Apify $0.13–0.20 per Compute Unit and a 20% commission | a Compute Unit costs about $0.01 to produce; the commission has no direct cost |
| Crawl and extraction API | page, priced by class (static, JavaScript, protected), with automatic escalation and a ceiling per request | billed only when the page passes its Page check, the run's declaration of what an expected page looks like | medians of $0.22, $0.60 and $1.67 per 1,000 | HTTP-first routing, Lightpanda, negotiated residential bandwidth |
| Hosted browsers | active browser-second, plus direct traffic, proxy gigabytes by kind and solves | standby not billed; headful live view priced with its egress | $0.08 per hour median; direct traffic $0.20/GB; residential about $7/GB | traffic, identity and solving, not the hour |
| AI agents | tokens at list plus a disclosed 15–20%, or a fixed price per verified task for a compiled Plan (the deterministic steps a first agent run produces, replayed later without the model) | first runs and Repairs (Plan fixes after a site changes) priced like the market | Browser Use at model cost +20%; $0.12–0.75 per 10-step task | replaying a compiled Plan cuts model tokens 3–6 times |

The crawl API's unit deserves one step more than the market takes. Competitors bill HTTP success, which often includes 404s and block pages served with a 200 status, or they bill result counts, and the team found no vendor billing per semantically verified result. Goodfetch's Verdicts, which a model never judges alone ([ADR 0013](../../../docs/adr/0013-ai-success-is-never-judged-by-a-model-alone.md)), and its benchmark per verified result on the Proving ground, the site Goodfetch owns where every page has a known expected result ([ADR 0025](../../../docs/adr/0025-benchmark-per-verified-result-on-the-proving-ground.md)), make a "verified page" or "verified record" auditable. Outcome pricing in AI support shows buyers pay for outcomes when the outcome is defined: Intercom charges $0.99 per resolution, and the disputes center on what counts as one ([Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/learning-center/ai-customer-service-agent-pricing-comparison)). Automatic escalation with a ceiling per request answers the multiplier complaints, as ScrapingBee's `mode=auto` tries configurations cheapest first, bills only the one that succeeds and accepts a `max_cost` ([ScrapingBee API contract](https://www.scrapingbee.com/llms/html-api.txt)). One flat price per page that bundles escalation, as Firecrawl charges $0.60–0.75 per 1,000 for every class at volume, is simpler, but the margin section shows it loses money on protected sites until Goodfetch has measured how many protected pages its HTTP route can serve.

For hosted browsers, the hour itself is in a price war, at $0.02 at Browser Use and $0.00 at Notte, so it should be billed per active second with a standby that bills nothing, as Kernel does, and the margin should come from what rides on it: direct and own-proxy traffic billed at $0.20/GB, as Browser Use and Anchor do ([Browser Use pricing](https://browser-use.com/pricing)), residential bytes, solves, session length, concurrency, and a separate price for headful Sessions (a full browser drawing a real screen, unlike a headless one) with live view, a real-time picture of the remote browser that a person can watch or steer; Kernel bills headful browsers at $0.48 an hour against $0.06 for headless ([Kernel pricing doc](https://www.kernel.sh/docs/info/pricing)). Competitors gate their strongest stealth to enterprise plans: Steel's stealth browser is Enterprise-only ([Steel pricing and limits](https://docs.steel.dev/overview/pricinglimits)), Hyperbrowser's "Ultra stealth" is "only available on enterprise plans" ([Hyperbrowser stealth](https://hyperbrowser.ai/docs/sessions/stealth.md)), and Browserbase keeps "Advanced" stealth and Verified Agents for its Scale plan ([Browserbase pricing](https://www.browserbase.com/pricing)), so the Goodfetch-built Chromium on self-serve plans is a visible difference. For agents, three structures coexist: tokens passed through with a disclosed markup (0% at Notte and Browserbase's Model Gateway, about 5% at Hyperbrowser, 20% at Browser Use, which charges 20% even when customers bring their own key), flat prices per step that include the model ($0.016 at TinyFish, $0.02 at HyperAgent), and runs included in plans ([Browser Use pricing](https://browser-use.com/pricing), [Hyperbrowser pricing](https://hyperbrowser.ai/docs/pricing), [TinyFish pricing](https://www.tinyfish.ai/pricing)). Agent runs should pass tokens through at list plus a disclosed 15–20%, and repeated work served by a compiled Plan should carry a fixed price per verified task, announced before the run, set below what an agent loop costs elsewhere and above what replay costs Goodfetch, so the replay saving becomes margin while the customer still pays less.

### The marketplace works only if Goodfetch owns the runtime and the bill

The Apify Store shows that this kind of marketplace carries real money: Apify reports "$1.6M paid out last month" across 4,500 community developers ([Apify partners](https://apify.com/partners/actor-developers)), up from more than CZK 24M, about $1M, for all of 2024 ([CzechCrunch](https://cc.cz/live/platforma-apify-loni-vyrostla-o-65-procenta-a-dosahla-obratu-300-milionu-korun/)). Since a developer's profit is "(0.8 * revenue) - platform costs" under pay-per-event pricing, where the developer charges per action or result ([Apify pricing and costs](https://docs.apify.com/platform/actors/publishing/monetize/pricing-and-costs)), those payouts imply at least $2M a month of Store billings. It works because the marketplace owns the runtime and the bill, so buyers and creators cannot settle outside it; RapidAPI, a listing layer without a runtime, ended with Nokia buying it on undisclosed terms after a $1B valuation ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/11/13/nokia-acquires-rapid-the-api-company-once-valued-at-1b/)). Apify retired flat monthly rentals on 1 October 2026 ([Apify rental docs](https://docs.apify.com/platform/actors/publishing/monetize/rental)), and a third-party census found 97.1% of 64,812 Store Actors priced per event ([apifystats](https://apifystats.com/stats.html)). Goodfetch should copy the proven point: **a 20% commission on pay-per-event Recipe revenue, net of the usage the Recipe's paying users consume, with that usage billed to the user separately, free users' usage absorbed by Goodfetch, and identity checks on every creator who is paid out**. It can seed supply with a zero or low commission on each creator's first revenue, as Shopify takes 0% of a developer's first $1M ([Shopify](https://shopify.dev/docs/apps/launch/distribution/revenue-share)). Other take rates, the share of each sale the marketplace keeps, bracket that choice: AWS Marketplace charges 3% on public SaaS listings and 1.5–2% on large private offers ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2024/01/aws-marketplace-simplified-reduced-listing-fees/)), and Shopify 15% above $1M.

### Be the merchant: Stripe direct with Connect

A **merchant of record** is a reseller that legally sells the product to the customer and handles VAT, fraud and chargebacks, for about 3.4–5% plus a fixed fee per transaction: Paddle charges 5% + 50¢ ([Paddle pricing](https://www.paddle.com/pricing)), and Stripe Managed Payments adds 3.5% to Stripe's card fees ([Stripe pricing](https://stripe.com/en-fr/pricing)). None can serve Goodfetch Cloud whole. Paddle prohibits products that let other sellers sell, "such as digital marketplaces", and lists "VPN and Proxies" as a restricted category ([Paddle policy](https://www.paddle.com/help/start/intro-to-paddle/what-am-i-not-allowed-to-sell-on-paddle)); Stripe Managed Payments requires selling "not through a platform or marketplace" ([Stripe eligibility](https://docs.stripe.com/payments/managed-payments/eligibility)). Goodfetch should therefore be its own merchant on Stripe, at about 1.5% + €0.25 for a standard EEA card plus 0.7% for Billing and 0.5% for Tax, and pay creators through Stripe Connect at €2 per active account and 0.25% + €0.10 per payout ([Stripe Connect pricing](https://stripe.com/en-fr/connect/pricing)). Stripe cannot hold real-time Limits: its meter events are processed asynchronously, and prepaid credit grants apply only when an invoice is finalized ([Stripe usage recording](https://docs.stripe.com/billing/subscriptions/usage-based/recording-usage-api), [Stripe billing credits](https://docs.stripe.com/billing/subscriptions/usage-based/billing-credits)). The live balance and the Limits therefore sit in Goodfetch's own ledger, and a rating engine turns hourly aggregates into invoices: Metronome, which Stripe now owns and recommends for new usage-based integrations ([Stripe newsroom](https://stripe.com/newsroom/news/stripe-completes-metronome-acquisition), [Stripe usage-based billing](https://docs.stripe.com/billing/subscriptions/usage-based)), or Stripe Billing's own meters for a simpler price book. AWS Marketplace adds an enterprise channel where customers spend committed AWS budgets, at 3% and with hourly usage reports ([AWS Marketplace metering](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/marketplace/latest/userguide/metering-for-usage.html)). This stays consistent with ADR 0045, under which Paid fetches to publishers come from the user's own wallet and Goodfetch never holds that money ([ADR 0045](../../../docs/adr/0045-payment-required-is-a-block-cause-and-paid-fetches-are-opt-in.md)).

### Trust and safety is part of the product

Reselling proxies and hosting other people's code makes Goodfetch accountable twice, to upstream suppliers and to AWS. Since 7 July 2026, Bright Data allows "no automatic, instant or self-serve path to the Residential network": new residential zones need a corporate email, funds on the account, a human review and possibly a video call ([Bright Data](https://docs.brightdata.com/proxy-networks/residential/network-access)), and Rayobyte blocks "Banks, government sites, login pages and sites that report abuse" for every customer ([Rayobyte](https://rayobyte.com/products/residential-proxies/)). Supply itself is fragile: the FBI seized NetNut on 2 July 2026 after it was tied to a botnet of at least two million devices, and Google said "many popular residential proxy brands are in fact whitelabeling the NetNut botnet" ([KrebsOnSecurity](https://krebsonsecurity.com/2026/07/fbi-seizes-netnut-proxy-platform-popa-botnet/)); Google had disrupted IPIDEA in January ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/01/google-disrupts-ipidea-one-of-worlds.html)). AWS's Acceptable Use Policy never names scraping, but it forbids using or facilitating others to use the services to violate "the security, integrity, or availability" of any system, and it weighs a customer's own prevention processes ([AWS AUP](https://aws.amazon.com/aup/)); an abuse notice left unanswered for 24 hours can get resources blocked or the account suspended ([AWS re:Post](https://repost.aws/knowledge-center/aws-abuse-report)). Know-your-customer (KYC) tiers, meaning identity and use-case checks (card and email for basic use, business verification for high concurrency, residential tiers and logged-in Sessions), an abuse desk, a log tying every egress address to a Tenant, run and domain, category blocks inherited from suppliers, and at least two upstream proxy vendors are therefore part of what is sold. US case law currently favors logged-out collection of public data ([Meta v. Bright Data](https://www.fbm.com/post/102kqw7/)), while fake accounts and terms breaches lose, as hiQ's $500,000 judgment showed ([Proskauer](https://newmedialaw.proskauer.com/2022/12/08/hiq-and-linkedin-reach-proposed-settlement-in-landmark-scraping-case/)); in the EU, the AI Act bans untargeted scraping of facial images for recognition databases ([AI Act Article 5](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/5/)), and the CNIL fined Kaspr €240,000 over scraped contact data ([CNIL](https://www.cnil.fr/en/data-scraping-kaspr-fined-eu240000)). One owner decision follows from this. ADR 0016 leaves robots.txt enforcement off by default ([ADR 0016](../../../docs/adr/0016-security-safe-by-default-compliance-switchable.md)), while a Cloudflare signed-agent listing requires obeying robots.txt and crawl delays ([Cloudflare signed agents](https://developers.cloudflare.com/bots/concepts/bot/signed-agents/)); I recommend a Goodfetch Cloud Operator policy (the settings an operator fixes for every run) that switches `respect_site_preferences` on for `identified` Sessions only, the Sessions that present a declared, signed agent identity, which keeps ADR 0016's defaults for standard and stealth runs and gives the signed identity a reputation worth having.

## Launch on AWS with Graviton, Karpenter and Firecracker, and keep the fleets portable

### AWS wins the launch; bare metal wins the steady fleet

No provider is both cheapest and best for every layer, so the answer has two parts. **For raw compute, AWS is among the dearest ways to buy browser-shaped capacity**: one unit of 1 vCPU (one hardware thread of a server processor) and 2 GB costs about $26.5 a month on Graviton, AWS's own ARM processors, at on-demand prices; Google Cloud costs the same and Azure 14% less, while European bare metal is 63–78% cheaper (Scaleway Elastic Metal, OVHcloud Scale, Hetzner AX), Hetzner's shared Arm cloud 89% cheaper, and Oracle Cloud's flexible shapes 48–64% cheaper with hyperscaler-style elasticity ([OVHcloud catalogue](https://api.ovh.com/1.0/order/catalog/public/baremetalServers?ovhSubsidiary=FR), [Scaleway Elastic Metal](https://www.scaleway.com/en/pricing/elastic-metal/), [Hetzner prices](https://docs.hetzner.com/general/infrastructure-and-availability/price-adjustment/), [OCI price list](https://apexapps.oracle.com/pls/apex/cetools/api/v1/products/?currencyCode=USD)). Networking widens the gap, because AWS bills data leaving it for the internet (egress) at $0.09/GB, while OVHcloud, Scaleway, Hetzner, DigitalOcean, Vultr and Latitude.sh include most traffic. At 10,000 always-on browsers, compute and network cost about $351k a month on AWS on-demand and $161k on Spot (spare capacity AWS sells at a deep discount and can reclaim at two minutes' notice), against $97k on OVHcloud, $71k on Scaleway and $117k on Hetzner; counting a hyperthread (half of a physical core) as 0.65 of a Graviton core brings bare metal roughly to parity with Spot, which live Sessions cannot use anyway. **For launching, AWS wins on what matters first**: managed databases, queues, schedulers and keys; startup credits (AWS Activate gives up to $200k with an investor's referral and more to invited AI startups, and the credits pay for Bedrock models but not for Marketplace purchases or the upfront fees of Savings Plans, AWS's term commitments that buy a discount ([AWS Activate](https://aws.amazon.com/startups/credits), [credit terms](https://aws.amazon.com/awscredits/))); per-second elasticity; and the footprint competitors already rely on. The alternatives carry their own risks: Hetzner raised prices by up to 37% in April 2026 and again in June ([heise](https://heise.de/-11185981), [Hetzner](https://docs.hetzner.com/general/infrastructure-and-availability/price-adjustment/)), its cloud forbids nested virtualization and its terms let it lock servers "without prior notice" ([Hetzner terms](https://www.hetzner.com/legal/terms-and-conditions/)); DigitalOcean's policy forbids collecting information "by deceit or covert means" ([DigitalOcean AUP](https://www.digitalocean.com/legal/acceptable-use-policy)); Cloudflare's Browser Run requests are "always identified as bot traffic" ([Cloudflare FAQ](https://developers.cloudflare.com/browser-rendering/faq/)); and Equinix Metal shut down on 30 June 2026 ([Equinix](https://docs.equinix.com/releases/end-of-life/eol-products-services)). Provider choice barely changes anti-bot outcomes, since IP-intelligence data classify both AWS's and Hetzner's networks as "Hosting" ([IPinfo AS16509](https://ipinfo.io/AS16509)).

The recommendation is to **launch everything on AWS, build the fleet layer to run on any Linux host with the KVM hypervisor from the first day (the engine is a static binary), and move the steady base of browser and Recipe capacity to European and US bare metal once concurrency passes roughly 1,000 peak browsers and its daily shape is known**, keeping AWS for the control plane, the model gateway and bursts; the margin section shows where the lines cross. Within AWS, the primary Regions should be us-east-1, the cheapest of the three priced and the one most competitors use, and eu-west-1 (Ireland), 6.9–12.9% dearer for compute but carrying every service this design needs; Paris is 11.9–18.8% dearer and lacks AMD and Graviton5 types ([AWS price list, eu-west-3](https://pricing.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/offers/v1.0/aws/AmazonEC2/20261009203134/eu-west-3/index.json)). Hosted browsers need Regions near their clients, because every action pays a round trip, while crawls and agents can run in the cheapest Region, since proxies set where a request appears to come from. EU data residency is met in standard EU Regions; the AWS European Sovereign Cloud, generally available since January 2026 in Brandenburg, is a separate partition with its own accounts, identity and billing ([AWS](https://press.aboutamazon.com/aws/2026/1/aws-launches-aws-european-sovereign-cloud-and-announces-expansion-across-europe)), and EU-owned providers with SecNumCloud-qualified offers, France's security qualification for cloud services ([OVHcloud](https://www.ovhcloud.com/en/compliance/secnumcloud/), [3DS Outscale](https://fr.outscale.com/secnumcloud/)), serve French public and regulated buyers, an option Apify and Browserbase do not appear to offer.

### A crawl run lands on a dense Graviton fleet that mostly runs on Spot

When a user launches a crawl or extraction run (what the market calls a scraper), the request reaches the API through CloudFront, AWS's content delivery network, and an Application Load Balancer, its HTTP-level load balancer, and becomes a message on SQS, AWS's managed message queue. SQS fair queues tag each message with its Tenant and serve quiet Tenants first when one floods the queue, with no change to the consumers ([SQS fair queues](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSSimpleQueueService/latest/SQSDeveloperGuide/sqs-fair-queues.html)). A dispatcher starts one engine process for the run, as ADR 0001 requires, on a densely packed fleet of **Graviton** instances (AWS's own ARM processors, the cheapest per vCPU) managed by **EKS**, AWS's hosted Kubernetes, and **Karpenter**, an open-source autoscaler that adds the cheapest suitable machines when work is waiting and removes them when it is not. Density is the point: in the lab a Rust HTTP fetch costs about 0.2–0.3 ms of CPU with an engine footprint of about 10 MiB ([lab: HTTP transports](../../lab/reports/http-transport-2026-10-09.md)), while Fargate, AWS's per-container service, has a minimum task of 0.25 vCPU and 0.5 GB ([Fargate task sizes](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonECS/latest/developerguide/fargate-tasks-services.html)). About 80% of this capacity should be **Spot**, which sold 43–80% below on-demand over the last 30 days and comes with a two-minute reclaim notice ([Spot Instance Advisor](https://aws.amazon.com/ec2/spot/instance-advisor/)); ADR 0002's Checkpoints, self-contained copies of a run's state, let the cloud send `migrate` on the notice and resume the run on another machine, redoing only the work since the last Checkpoint ([ADR 0002](../../../docs/adr/0002-portable-run-state-resume-from-checkpoints.md)). Karpenter replaces Spot nodes with other Spot nodes only when it may choose among at least 15 instance types ([Karpenter disruption](https://karpenter.sh/docs/concepts/disruption/)). The run's live SQLite state stays on the host's local disk, because SQLite's write-ahead log needs a single host ([SQLite WAL](https://www.sqlite.org/wal.html)); Checkpoints go to S3, AWS's object storage, with conditional writes, which succeed only if nothing changed since the last read, keyed by the run epoch that marks which copy of a run is current, giving ADR 0003 its fence ([S3 conditional writes](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/conditional-writes.html), [ADR 0003](../../../docs/adr/0003-two-level-fencing-at-least-once-delivery.md)); and results land in S3 as multi-megabyte chunks, never as one object per Record. Hosts sit in public subnets with their own public IPv4 and IPv6 addresses, never behind a NAT Gateway (see the defaults below), and a limiter per target domain in Valkey, AWS's managed in-memory store, keeps fifty Tenants' runs on the same site polite together. A run starts in milliseconds on a node with room, while a new node takes from about 30 seconds to a few minutes, so the fleet scales on queue backlog and keeps headroom.

### A Recipe run gets its own Firecracker microVM on Graviton bare metal

A Recipe is user code, so it needs a VM boundary. Each Recipe run should get a Firecracker microVM, in the E2B pattern, on Graviton **bare-metal (`.metal`) instances**: whole physical servers that let Goodfetch run its own VMs with Linux's KVM hypervisor at full speed, priced the same per vCPU as virtual sizes ([AWS price list, us-east-1](https://pricing.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/offers/v1.0/aws/AmazonEC2/20261009203134/us-east-1/index.json)); every platform Firecracker lists as tested is a `.metal` instance ([Firecracker](https://github.com/firecracker-microvm/firecracker)). Defense in depth follows from the advisories above: Firecracker's jailer with a tight system-call filter, no runtime configuration driven by annotations (several Kata escapes came that way), no caches shared across Tenants (Windmill's sandbox escape poisoned shared worker caches ([advisory](https://github.com/windmill-labs/windmill/security/advisories/GHSA-2jfj-x8j7-7mfh))), an egress firewall on the host that the code inside cannot change, instance metadata blocked, and no AWS credentials inside any run: a host agent uploads Checkpoints under a session policy scoped to that Tenant and run. The alternatives cost more or protect less. Since 16 February 2026 AWS supports **nested virtualization**, running one's own VMs inside an ordinary AWS VM, but only on Intel 7th- and 8th-generation types, and AWS recommends bare metal for performance-sensitive work ([EC2 nested virtualization](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSEC2/latest/UserGuide/amazon-ec2-nested-virtualization.html)); in the team's cost model it costs 26–69% more per Session than Graviton `.metal`. Kata Containers, which wraps each pod in a light VM, adds 130 MiB and a quarter of a vCPU per pod on EKS ([AWS Containers blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/containers/enhancing-kubernetes-workload-isolation-and-security-using-kata-containers/)), and gVisor, which intercepts system calls in a user-space kernel, runs on any instance but is a weaker boundary than a VM. Lambda MicroVMs, a new AWS product (the team could not confirm its launch date) that offers Firecracker isolation, snapshot starts, suspend and an inbound WebSocket per VM, needs no fleet, but it costs about 3.2 times Fargate per running hour, is documented for Arm only (its price list also carries x86 rates), caps a VM's life at 8 hours and defaults to 5 launches per second ([Lambda MicroVMs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/lambda/latest/dg/lambda-microvms-guide.html), [Lambda quotas](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/lambda/latest/dg/gettingstarted-limits.html)); it is an escape hatch for spiky early load. Lambda Managed Instances let one container serve several invocations at once, which rules them out for Tenant code ([Lambda Managed Instances](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/lambda/latest/dg/lambda-managed-instances.html)).

### A browser Session claims a pre-booted microVM and starts Chromium after the claim

When code or an agent asks for a hosted browser, the Session should come from a **warm pool**, capacity kept ready so a request never waits for a machine, of pre-booted microVMs in the nearest Region. The pool must hold sandboxes, not running browsers: the Goodfetch-built Chromium takes its fingerprint seed as a launch flag, so a snapshot of a launched browser would give every clone the same identity, and Firecracker itself calls resuming one state more than once "insecure" ([Firecracker snapshots](https://github.com/firecracker-microvm/firecracker/blob/main/docs/snapshotting/snapshot-support.md)). Chromium therefore starts after the claim, which costs about 0.1–0.5 s and puts Goodfetch near Browser Use's 825 ms median and Browserbase's figure of under 2 seconds rather than Kernel's 30 ms. The Browser host, the Goodfetch process that runs beside each hosted browser, carries out one high-level action per network round trip ([ADR 0022](../../../docs/adr/0022-remote-browsers-driven-through-a-browser-host.md)). The client receives a Session URL naming its host and a short-lived token; its connection enters a **Network Load Balancer**, AWS's connection-level load balancer, on a TCP listener whose idle timeout reaches 6,000 s (its TLS listener is fixed at 350 s) ([NLB idle timeout](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/elasticloadbalancing/latest/network/update-idle-timeout.html)), and then a Goodfetch session gateway written in Rust that terminates TLS, checks the token, looks up which host owns the Session, forwards the bytes and meters them per Tenant. AWS's managed WebSocket gateway cannot do this job: it closes connections after 2 hours or 10 idle minutes and refuses messages over 128 KB, which screenshots exceed ([API Gateway WebSocket quotas](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/apigateway/latest/developerguide/apigateway-execution-service-websocket-limits-table.html)). A live page cannot move between machines, so interactive Sessions run on on-demand capacity covered by **Savings Plans**, one- or three-year commitments to spend a fixed dollar amount per hour in exchange for 26–58% off, and only batch browser crawls use Spot. Running each Session in its own Firecracker microVM on `.metal` costs about the same per slot as a plain instance (on-demand $0.075 against $0.074 an hour for a rich headless Session in the team's model), because the VM overhead is a few megabytes. The pool scales on headroom, idle ready Sessions against the arrival rate, rather than on CPU, and new-account quotas (6 Fargate vCPUs, 1,000 concurrent Lambda executions, 5 MicroVM launches per second) must be raised before launch. Idle agent Sessions should drop into a snapshot standby that bills nothing, Kernel's pattern. Fargate is a poor home for the stealth browser in any case, because it cannot enlarge shared memory or relax the system-call filter, so Chromium's own sandbox is expected to fail there and leave the task's VM as the only boundary against a hostile page ([Fargate restrictions](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonECS/latest/developerguide/fargate-tasks-services.html), [Puppeteer troubleshooting](https://pptr.dev/troubleshooting)). One question needs a lab check before stealth fleets commit to Graviton: Chrome for Testing now ships Linux arm64 builds ([Chrome for Testing](https://googlechromelabs.github.io/chrome-for-testing/)) and ADR 0035 builds arm64 Chromium, but a Linux-on-ARM browser can reveal its architecture to sites, and that is a rare desktop profile.

### An agent run waits on models, so memory and model routing set its cost

An agent run holds a browser for 1 to 11 minutes per task while mostly waiting on model calls ([Browser Use](https://browser-use.com/posts/speed-matters), [Stagehand evals](https://www.stagehand.dev/evals)), so it is memory-bound and fits the same pools with CPU overcommitted. Its real cost is tokens, and the model route matters more than the compute. Claude on Bedrock, AWS's model-hosting service, lacks Message Batches, server-side tools and the August 2026 computer-use and browser toolsets ([Claude in Amazon Bedrock](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/claude-in-amazon-bedrock)), and Bedrock lists no batch prices for the 5.5 models ([AWS Bedrock price list](https://pricing.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/offers/v1.0/aws/AmazonBedrockFoundationModels/20261008235637/us-east-1/index.json)), so agent model calls should go to Anthropic's first-party API or Claude Platform on AWS, which bills through AWS Marketplace at first-party rates ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). Search should go through a SERP API at $0.30–1.00 per 1,000 queries ([Serper](https://serper.dev/)) rather than model-native search at $10–14 per 1,000 plus tokens, and prompts should be built around cache hits, which cost 2.5–10% of the input price: the 6,600-token Claude browser toolset costs $0.0132 per uncached Sonnet 5.5 call and $0.00066 as a cache read. AWS's own AgentCore, a managed runtime and browser service for agents, bills only active CPU ($0.0895 per vCPU-hour) and beats Fargate below about 23% average CPU ([AgentCore pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/bedrock/agentcore/pricing/)), but AgentCore Browser runs AWS's Chrome, so it cannot carry the stealth build unless AWS accepts custom browsers, which nobody has confirmed. Schedules need no special compute: EventBridge Scheduler, with 10 million schedules per Region and 1,000 invocations per second by default ([EventBridge Scheduler quotas](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/scheduler/latest/UserGuide/scheduler-quotas.html)), should only fire messages into SQS with a run ID built from the schedule and the occurrence time, while ADR 0036's semantics stay in Goodfetch's scheduler ([ADR 0036](../../../docs/adr/0036-the-daemon-schedules-runs-above-the-engine.md)).

### Defaults that would quietly erase the margin

A handful of AWS defaults would cost more than the compute they serve. A **NAT Gateway**, the managed device that lets machines without public addresses reach the internet, charges $0.045 for every gigabyte it processes in either direction ([NAT Gateway pricing](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/vpc/latest/userguide/nat-gateway-pricing.html)). Because inbound traffic through an ordinary internet gateway is free, a crawler fleet behind a NAT Gateway pays about $45 per terabyte for bytes that would otherwise cost nothing, about $18k a month at 1,000 browsers, so NAT belongs only on the low-volume control-plane path, where customers allowlist fixed addresses. Public IPv4 addresses cost $0.005 an hour each, in use or idle ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/new-aws-public-ipv4-address-charge-public-ip-insights/)), and rotating them buys no reputation: AWS publishes its address ranges ([AWS IP ranges](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/vpc/latest/userguide/aws-ip-ranges.html)), and Cloudflare kept fingerprinting Perplexity's crawler across rotated addresses and networks ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/perplexity-is-using-stealth-undeclared-crawlers-to-evade-website-no-crawl-directives/)). Egress should instead run in three tiers: direct from AWS, or from Goodfetch's own address block, for unprotected targets; a signed identity under **Web Bot Auth**, a standard in which a bot signs each request with a published key, registered with Cloudflare as an intermediary agent, as AWS's AgentCore Browser already does ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2025/10/amazon-bedrock-agentcore-browser-web-bot-auth-preview)); and partner **residential proxies** (traffic routed through opted-in consumer devices' home connections) or ISP proxies (static addresses leased from consumer internet providers) for protected targets, since headless Chromium on a datacenter address passed only 2% of protected sites in Browser Use's vendor-run test ([Browser Use stealth benchmark](https://browser-use.com/posts/stealth-benchmark)). Observability is the next trap: CloudWatch charges $0.30 a month per metric and dimension combination and $0.50/GB for logs ([CloudWatch pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/cloudwatch/pricing/)), so per-Tenant dimensions for 10,000 Tenants and five usage kinds would create 50,000 metrics costing about $7,000 a month at AWS's tiered rates, and 10 TB of browser logs $5,000 against $1,000–1,500 at Better Stack ([Better Stack pricing](https://betterstack.com/pricing)). Streaming services carry floors (Kinesis On-demand Advantage bills at least 25 MB/s, about $3,100 a month ([Kinesis pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/kinesis/data-streams/pricing/)), and MSK Serverless about $548 a month per cluster), Firehose rounds every record up to 5 KB ([Firehose pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/firehose/pricing/)), and Aurora's auto-pause adds 15–30 s to the first query after a pause ([Aurora auto-pause](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonRDS/latest/AuroraUserGuide/aurora-serverless-v2-auto-pause.html)). Accounts are a blast-radius tool as well: the egress-heavy fleets belong in their own AWS accounts per Region, so a Trust & Safety block on a runner account cannot stop the API, the Datasets or invoicing ([AWS multi-account guidance](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/whitepapers/latest/organizing-your-aws-environment/organizing-your-aws-environment.html)).

Three billing families dominate the crawl market. Each business makes money in a different place, and none of them on the thing it seems to sell. **Hosted browsers** earn 19.6% on the bare hour at the $0.08 median, and lose 77% on headful Sessions with live view, but billing non-proxied traffic at $0.20/GB lifts the browser line to 67.8%, residential bytes earn 72% and solves 84%, and headful Sessions earn 71% at Kernel's headful price; the hour is the meter on which traffic, identity, solving and agent work are sold. **The crawl and extraction API** earns 93–99% on static and JavaScript pages even at volume prices, but protected pages are the volume trap at 4.6%; routing 70% of them over HTTP with the Session's cookies cuts their cost to $0.93 per 1,000 and the margin to 44%, and a residential contract at $1/GB on top takes it to 71%. **Recipe hosting and the marketplace** earn 93% on the Compute Unit at Apify's lowest price, and the 20% commission has no direct cost; the ceiling is set by agent sandbox vendors selling compute at about $0.03 per Compute Unit-equivalent, which caps how long a $0.13–0.20 Compute Unit price can hold. **Agents** earn 41–43% on mid and frontier models when a loop runs every time, and 83–85% at the same price when a compiled Plan replays, so replay turns agent work from an AI-margin product into an infrastructure-margin one. Resold tokens are a pass-through that loses money after the roughly 3.8% of payment fees unless marked up by at least 4%, which is why the recommended markup is 15–20%.

### Overheads take 13–31 points, and trust and safety is the largest at small scale

The model blends four illustrative revenue mixes, deducts payment fees and creator payouts (about 3.8% of revenue on Stripe direct), the free tier (three free active users per payer using half of a $5 allowance, ×1.5 for abuse), AWS Support, observability, a trust-and-safety desk (1.5 full-time staff at $8,500 a month, plus one per 4,000 payers) and the control plane in two production Regions plus staging (about $2,600–2,800 per Region a month).

| Revenue mix | Gross margin before overheads | After payments, payouts and free tier | After all overheads at $100k a month | After all overheads at $1M a month |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Proxy-heavy (45% proxies, 15% crawl, 12% browsers) | 59.0% | 50.2% | 29.4% | 46.2% |
| Compute-heavy (30% Compute Units, 25% browsers, 20% crawl) | 56.9% | 47.8% | 26.2% | 42.9% |
| LLM-heavy (35% agents, 25% resold tokens) | 45.1% | 34.8% | 13.9% | 30.7% |
| Marketplace-heavy (40% Recipe event revenue, 25% Compute Units) | 51.1% | 41.3% | 20.7% | 37.7% |

Before overheads, the blend sits where Twilio (49%, with carrier pass-through fees in its cost of revenue) and Fastly (57.1%) do, below DigitalOcean (about 60%) and Cloudflare (74.5%) ([Twilio 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1447669/000144766926000021/twlo-20251231.htm), [Fastly results](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260211703867/en/Fastly-Announces-Both-Record-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-2025-Financial-Results), [DigitalOcean results](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260224714253/en), [Cloudflare results](https://cloudflare.net/news/news-details/2026/Cloudflare-Announces-Fourth-Quarter-and-Fiscal-Year-2025-Financial-Results/default.aspx)), and inside the 50–60% band a16z observed for AI companies ([a16z](https://a16z.com/the-new-business-of-ai-and-how-its-different-from-traditional-software/)). After overheads it falls below every public benchmark, partly because those companies book trust and safety and support outside gross margin; on a basis closer to their filings, leaving the desk and the control plane out, the mixes earn 34–49% at $1M a month. Fixed cloud costs come to about $21k a month at 1,000 payers and $44k at 10,000, and the trust-and-safety desk alone costs 12.8 points of margin at $100k of monthly revenue and 2.2 points at $1M, which is why scale matters more than any AWS discount early on. A merchant of record would cost another 1.0–3.4 points, and can only cover the non-marketplace flows in any case. Counting marketplace revenue net of creator payouts, as many marketplaces report it, the marketplace-heavy mix earns 75.1% before overheads.

### Four levers lift the blend, but price and free usage move it most

The published model prices everything at the market median. To answer what the recommended business model earns, I ran the same model with its own functions and one input changed per step, each step keeping the previous ones: browser traffic billed at $0.20/GB as Browser Use and Anchor do; resold tokens marked up 15% instead of the 2.5% median; 70% of protected pages served over HTTP instead of 40%; residential bandwidth contracted at $1.00/GB instead of $2.00 (SOAX publishes enterprise floors of $0.35–0.85 and Rayobyte "from $0.50/GB" ([Rayobyte pricing](https://rayobyte.com/pricing/))); and one free active user per payer, close to Apify's ratio at the end of 2025 (56,000 active users for 27,000 payers), instead of three.

| Case (each adds to the one above) | Average of the four mixes after all overheads, $100k a month | Same, $1M a month |
|---|---|---|
| A. Market-median prices, as published | 22.5% | 39.4% |
| B. Direct browser traffic billed at $0.20/GB | 30.4% | 47.4% |
| C. Tokens marked up 15% | 31.3% | 48.3% |
| D. 70% of protected pages served over HTTP | 34.0% | 51.0% |
| E. Residential bandwidth at $1.00/GB | 39.4% | 56.5% |
| F. One free active user per payer instead of three | 41.8% | 58.9% |

Cases B and C are pricing choices Goodfetch controls today, case D depends on an HTTP-route share nobody has measured on real protected sites, and case E on a supplier contract. At $1M a month with all four levers, the proxy-heavy and compute-heavy mixes reach 66% and the LLM-heavy and marketplace-heavy mixes 47% after all overheads, and 59–77% before them, so the blend approaches DigitalOcean's roughly 60% rather than Twilio's 49%. The published one-at-a-time sensitivity ranks the drivers by their swing in the four-mix average at $1M a month: price position moves it 22.1 points (25.1% at 0.7 times the median, 47.2% at 1.25 times), free active users per payer 15.9 points (42.9% at one, 27.1% at ten), residential cost 10.1 points, browser fleet utilization 9.2 points (32.7% at 40%, 41.9% at 85%), and bytes per protected browser load 8.8 points. **The Spot share of batch capacity moves it only 1.9 points**, and the token markup 1.4 points on average (4.6 on the LLM-heavy mix): proxy economics together move the blend by about 25 points, so the HTTP-first engine and a negotiated residential contract are worth more than every AWS purchase option combined. The sensitivity varies one input at a time and does not model a price war that lowers prices and raises free usage together, or a residential price rise after supply shocks like NetNut's seizure.

### A hybrid fleet beats all-AWS above about 1,000 concurrent browsers

The model's fleet comparison follows rich headless Sessions with 30% of hours carrying live-view traffic, a daily demand curve averaging 69% of peak, revenue at $0.08 per browser-hour with proxies and models excluded, and for the hybrids a bare-metal base sized at 60% of peak plus $8,000 a month and $50 per node of operations staff, an assumption since nobody has quantified that cost.

**At 100 concurrent browsers AWS wins clearly**, because one OVHcloud server holds 326 browsers and the fixed operations cost dominates; at 1,000 the options converge within about 10%; at 10,000 a bare-metal base with on-demand burst, the like-for-like layout for live Sessions, costs $141–151k a month against $210k for AWS's best interactive layout, and batch Sessions with Spot burst cost $99–128k. Counting a hyperthread as 0.65 of a Graviton core raises the OVHcloud hybrid at 10,000 peak from $112k to $144k and Hetzner's from $128k to $167k, both still below AWS's $210k. Crawl fleets favour the hybrid even more, since their compute is a tiny share of cost and included bandwidth avoids AWS egress. The hybrid's risks are the ones priced nowhere in the model: Hetzner's 2026 price rises, unverified stock for the roughly 31 OVHcloud or 124 Hetzner servers a 10,000-browser base needs, and bare-metal staffing, for which the only public data point is 37signals' claim of leaving AWS "without adding any new staff" while saving about $10M over five years ([37signals](https://basecamp.com/cloud-exit)). AWS credits, ignored by the model, tilt the first 12–24 months further toward AWS.

### Funnel scenarios reach $2–39M of ARR by month 36

The model's three 36-month scenarios combine sign-up volumes, growth and enterprise pace that are assumptions with conversion rates from ChartMogul's 2026 benchmark (median free-to-paid conversion of 8% within six months; "good" freemium 3–5%) ([ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/)). New self-serve payers split 60% hobbyists at $25 a month, 28% agent developers at $120 and 12% data teams at $450, and enterprise logos pay $5,000 a month; each segment carries one revenue mix's margin. They are scenarios, not forecasts.

| Scenario | Month-1 sign-ups, conversion, price position | ARR at month 12 | ARR at month 24 | ARR at month 36 | Payers at month 36 | Month gross profit first covers fixed cloud costs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative | 800, 3%, 25% below the median | $0.28M | $1.01M | $2.11M | about 1,000 | 20 |
| Base | 2,000, 5%, the median | $1.86M | $6.77M | $15.1M | about 6,800 | 6 |
| Upside | 3,000, 7%, the median | $4.47M | $16.8M | $38.9M | about 18,800 | 4 |

Gross profit stays at 38–46% of revenue across the scenarios, after payments and the free tier, because the segment mixes carry the browser, proxy and token lines at market prices; the fixed cloud costs it covers exclude engineering, sales and administration, so those months are not company break-even. The upside tracks Bessemer's "Shooting Star" curve of about $3M, $12M and $40M of ARR in years one to three, a little faster in year one ([Bessemer](https://www.bvp.com/atlas/the-state-of-ai-2025)). The base case reaches about half of Apify's $31M with about a quarter of its 27,000 payers, at a blended $185 a month per payer against Apify's roughly $96, so it leans more on data teams and enterprise than Apify does. The revenue pools are real but uneven: access networks are the largest and most profitable (Bright Data above $300M, Oxylabs $350M), the Apify Store moves at least $24M a year of creator-priced work, Firecrawl's job ads claim an eight-figure ARR in its first year ([Firecrawl job ad](https://haystackapp.io/jobs/6690a216-14d3-407a-ba74-4066b1b843a1)), and hosted-browser revenue is barely disclosed (Browserbase's 2025 ARR of about $4.4M is a low-confidence third-party estimate ([GetLatka](https://getlatka.com/companies/browserbase.com))). Nothing yet ties GitHub stars or installs to paid conversion, so the funnel inputs need interviews and a price test before anyone treats a scenario as a plan.

## Conclusion

The research moves the decisive question from the cloud bill to the engine. AWS purchase options, the most technical-looking choice, move the blended margin least, under 2 points for the whole Spot policy, while bytes and routing move it by about 25 points and price and free usage by 16–22. Goodfetch's pricing power therefore sits in what its engine already does differently: HTTP-first routing that keeps protected pages out of the browser, Lightpanda where pages allow it, compiled Plans that remove most model calls, and Verdicts that can make "verified result" a billable unit no competitor sells. The bare browser-hour is a commodity in a price war and should be treated as the meter on which traffic, identity, solving and agent work are sold, with stealth on self-serve plans as the visible difference that competitors keep for enterprise.

Two further implications follow. The provider decision has an expiry date set by concurrency rather than by time: AWS is the right home while the control plane and the trust-and-safety desk dominate fixed costs and credits pay for experiments, but a steady fleet beyond roughly 1,000 concurrent browsers is cheaper on European bare metal, and keeping fleets portable costs little now and a great deal later. And the supply chain behind the product is regulated in practice: Bright Data's KYC, the NetNut seizure and AWS's 24-hour abuse rule make trust and safety a fixed cost that sinks small-scale margins and a moat once it exists. What the evidence cannot settle is the size and shape of the browser fleet, because nobody has measured the Goodfetch-built Chromium's memory on Linux, real fleet utilization, the bytes a protected page costs or how often challenges appear; those four numbers will move the margin more than any price list in this report.
