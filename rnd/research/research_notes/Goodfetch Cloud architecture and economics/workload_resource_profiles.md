## 1. Headless Chromium: memory per process and per tab or context, CPU per page, start-up, sessions per vCPU and per GB, Lightpanda versus Chrome, resource blocking, practical concurrency and memory growth

### Takeaway
A Chrome page costs roughly 0.1 to 0.3 CPU-seconds per load on light pages (more on heavy pages and under contention). An extra tab adds roughly 16 to 30 MB on blank or light pages, on top of a base process that ranges from about 50 MB (chrome-headless-shell, idle) to more than 1 GB (full Chrome during a crawl). Vendors plan for about 0.3 to 0.8 GB and 0.16 to 0.4 vCPU per concurrent session (2.5 to 6 sessions per vCPU). On light JavaScript pages a 4 vCPU instance stops gaining throughput at about 5 tabs (about 20 pages/s) and collapses at 100. Lightpanda uses about 10 to 20 times less CPU and 15 to 65 times less memory than Chrome, but only on pages it can handle.

**Vendor sizing guidance (sessions per vCPU and per GB)**
- Browserless Enterprise best practices: light workload (5 to 10 concurrent) needs 2 CPU and 4 GB RAM; medium (10 to 20) needs 4 CPU and 8 GB; heavy (20 to 50) needs 8+ CPU and 16 GB+. Other advice: QUEUED typically 1.5 to 2x CONCURRENT (default 10); 2 GB of shared memory (`--shm-size=2g`); scale out with more containers rather than raising CONCURRENT; reuse one browser connection with several pages. `MAX_MEMORY_PERCENT`/`MAX_CPU_PERCENT` health checks only reject new sessions, and a running session's memory growth can trigger the OOM killer. No per-session memory figure is given — [Browserless best practices](https://docs.browserless.io/enterprise/docker/best-practices)
- Researcher arithmetic on those tiers: 0.16 to 0.4 vCPU and 0.32 to 0.8 GB per concurrent session, or 2.5 to 6.2 sessions per vCPU — derived from [Browserless best practices](https://docs.browserless.io/enterprise/docker/best-practices)
- Browserless's Docker config reference suggests starting CONCURRENT at 5 to 10 and raising it with measurement. Its doc pages disagree on the default (5 versus 10) (via search summary, not fetched) — [Browserless Docker config](https://docs.browserless.io/docker/config)

**Memory growth over long sessions and recycling practice**
- Crawlee's browser pool (v3.18.2) defaults: `maxOpenPagesPerBrowser = 20`, `retireBrowserAfterPageCount = 100`, `closeInactiveBrowserAfterSecs = 300`, `operationTimeoutSecs = 15`. The code comment justifies retirement by browsers getting bloated after many pages, without numbers — [crawlee browser-pool.ts](https://github.com/apify/crawlee/blob/v3.18.2/packages/browser-pool/src/browser-pool.ts)
- Goodfetch ADR 0040 recycles a browser after 100 pages, when its memory passes a per-browser cap (by default twice its post-warm-up size), or after idling. It counts Chrome by PSS on Linux. It also states that a run moving to the browser tier takes about a hundred times the memory at the same concurrency — [ADR 0040](/Users/jb/Dev/automan/docs/adr/0040-one-resource-governor-per-engine-sized-from-measured-load.md)
- In the published crawler benchmark, a single Chrome tab crawling 933 pages in sequence reached a 1.3 GB PSS peak, the same as 2 tabs. That is consistent with accumulation within one process, but the benchmark does not isolate growth — [BENCHMARKS.md](https://github.com/lightpanda-io/demo/blob/main/BENCHMARKS.md)

### Inferences
- **Practical concurrency per instance.** On a 4 vCPU / 16 GB instance (m5.xlarge), Chrome stopped gaining throughput at 5 or more tabs, at about 20 light pages/s, using about 2.4 to 2.5 cores. At 100 tabs it lost about a third of its throughput and used 4.2 GB. Browserless's tiers point to the same order: 10 to 20 sessions on 4 vCPU / 8 GB and 20 to 50 on 8 vCPU / 16 GB. For a 16 vCPU instance no published figure was found. Extrapolating the Browserless ratio gives roughly 40 to 100 concurrent agent-style sessions, which spend most of their time idle. For page-throughput crawling, it gives roughly 4x the 4 vCPU plateau (about 80 light pages/s). Both are extrapolations, not measurements.
- **CPU per 1,000 browser pages.** About 116 to 127 ms of CPU per light JS page works out to about 0.032 to 0.035 vCPU-hours per 1,000 pages, or about 28,000 to 31,000 pages per vCPU-hour at saturation. A heavier local e-commerce page (about 294 ms) gives about 0.08 vCPU-hours per 1,000 pages. Real heavy single-page apps were not measured by any source found.
- **Session-hour sizing.** Agent sessions are mostly idle between model steps (Browser Use says browsers spend most of their time waiting), so a browser-hour is better modelled as memory held (about 0.3 to 0.8 GB at vendor sizing, or 1.5 GB to 8 GB at the microVM vendors) than as CPU used. The CPU share per session-hour is 0.16 to 0.4 vCPU under Browserless sizing.
- **Base cost depends on the build.** The base process cost differs by an order of magnitude between chrome-headless-shell (about 50 MB idle) and full new-headless Chrome. Measured on macOS, full Chrome was about 0.8 GB idle and 1.1 to 1.4 GB in lab runs, so which Chromium build the Goodfetch stealth browser is based on matters for density. This is inferred from macOS footprint data and needs Linux PSS confirmation.
- **Pre-warming.** It removes about 70 to 250 ms of local process launch (or about 180 ms of container start), and 0.5 to 1.7 s of hosted-API session lifecycle. The cost is the idle memory per pooled browser (about 39 MiB measured for a headless-shell container). Snapshot-based microVMs advertise 20 to 50 ms resume, which is vendor-claimed.

### Gaps
- No controlled, version-pinned measurement of Chrome memory per tab on a typical page or a heavy single-page app was found. Only blank tabs, light demo pages and anecdotal 150 to 500 MB figures are available.
- No public per-session vCPU or memory specification from Browserbase, Steel or Kernel was found, apart from Kernel's 8 GB headful figure from a third-party case study.
- No controlled measurement of the CPU or latency saved by blocking images, fonts and media was found.
- No quantitative growth curve of Chrome memory over hours-long sessions was found.
- There is no measurement on 8 or 16 vCPU instances.
- The researcher's launch and footprint measurements ran on a loaded macOS host. The bare-Linux numbers (PSS, cgroup peak) the lab plans are still missing.

## 2. Goodfetch's own lab measurements: per-task CPU, RSS, throughput and start-up, with host and conditions

### Takeaway
Every lab number below comes from an Apple M3 Max Mac on local fixture pages. The lab itself calls them indicative, not publishable. They are still consistent with the external data. A browser task costs about 0.8 to 1.3 CPU-seconds with CDP drivers (2 to 3 s with Playwright) and holds about 1.1 to 1.4 GB on Chrome, versus 52 to 177 ms and 17 to 162 MB on Lightpanda. An HTTP fetch with reqwest or wreq costs about 0.2 to 0.3 ms of CPU and about 10 MB of RSS. A learned HTTP or API route runs a page at 0.4 to 0.8% of the browser's time.

### Inferences
- **Lab figures as upper bounds.** The lab's Chrome CPU per job (0.8 to 3.1 s) is about 6 to 28 times the external steady-state figure (about 0.11 to 0.13 s per light page). The lab metric amortizes browser start, conformance and warm-up over few tasks (about 7 attempts per cell, 5 of them measured), and mixes in multi-action forms and clicks. Treat it as an upper bound for short sessions, not a per-page cost.
- **Ratios to anchor a cost model.** At the same concurrency, the browser tier holds about 100 to 350 times the memory of a reqwest/wreq fetch (1.1 to 1.4 GB versus 4 to 10 MB) and uses about 3,000 to 5,000 times the CPU per page (about 1 s versus 0.2 to 0.3 ms). Main-content extraction (about 10 to 15 ms) costs 50 to 70 times more CPU than the fetch itself. Model the HTTP tier's CPU per 1,000 pages as about 15 s for extraction plus about 0.3 s for fetching (about 0.004 vCPU-hours), before TLS and decompression of real pages.
- **Routing is the largest cost lever.** Each page moved from the browser to a learned HTTP or API route cuts compute time by two orders of magnitude.

### Gaps
- No bare-Linux reference-host figures yet: no PSS or cgroup memory peaks for Chrome, and no CPU of terminated children.
- No lab measurement of browser launch time on its own; `startup-ms` excludes browser launch.
- No lab campaign at concurrency above 4 for HTTP, so per-transport plateaus are unknown.
- No lab measurement of TLS handshake CPU or of real-network HTTP fetches (the fixtures are loopback).
- The lab measures no heavy single-page app or real JS-built page; the WCXB corpus holds post-render snapshots without subresources.

## 3. HTTP crawling: pages per second per vCPU for Rust, Go, Python and Node crawlers, and politeness-limited throughput in practice

### Takeaway
Raw HTTP fetching is cheap: a fraction of a millisecond of CPU per page for Rust clients on loopback. Scrapy's no-op benchmark reaches about 50 pages/s in one Python process, and Colly claims more than 1,000 requests/s per core. In practice, throughput is bounded by politeness, about 1 request/s per domain under Scrapy's current defaults, and by parsing and extraction CPU (about 10 to 15 ms per page). Public cross-language benchmarks are weak and not comparable.

### Cited Findings
- **Goodfetch lab, Rust and Node.** Steady-state CPU per page fetched on loopback, from the open-arrival run, works out to about 0.19 ms (reqwest), 0.22 ms (wreq), 0.31 ms (Impit), 0.31 ms (Node), 0.53 ms (Spider) and 3.8 ms (pooled Crawlee) (researcher division of CPU per task by 8 pages). That is roughly 1,900 to 5,300 pages per busy core-second for Rust clients, before TLS and real page sizes — [http-transport-2026-10-09.md](/Users/jb/Dev/automan/rnd/lab/reports/http-transport-2026-10-09.md)
- **Scrapy (Python).** `scrapy bench`, with a local HTTP server and a spider that only follows links, crawls about 3,000 pages per minute (2,880 pages/min in the example; 1,920 to 4,200 during the run). Real spiders that do more processing crawl more slowly. No hardware is stated — [Scrapy benchmarking](https://docs.scrapy.org/en/latest/topics/benchmarking.html)
- **Colly (Go).** The README claims more than 1,000 requests/s on a single core, with no benchmark setup — [gocolly/colly](https://github.com/gocolly/colly)
- **Spider (Rust).** Its repository benchmark crawls `rsseau.fr` (185 pages, 10 samples) in 73 ms with Spider, 32 s with Colly, 15 s with Node.js crawler and 70 s with wget, on an M1 Max with 10 cores and 64 GB. On a 2-core Linux runner the times are 50 ms, 30 s, 3.4 s and 60 s. No versions or date are given, and the 400x gap with Colly suggests different politeness or concurrency defaults, not transport speed (researcher reading) — [spider BENCHMARKS.md](https://github.com/spider-rs/spider/blob/main/benches/BENCHMARKS.md)
- **Crawlee (Node).** Its README gives no throughput figure — [apify/crawlee](https://github.com/apify/crawlee). The lab measured pooled Crawlee at 30 to 70 ms of CPU and 245 to 512 MiB per 4- or 8-page task — [http-transport-2026-10-09.md](/Users/jb/Dev/automan/rnd/lab/reports/http-transport-2026-10-09.md)
- **Politeness defaults.** Scrapy's current settings defaults are `CONCURRENT_REQUESTS = 16`, `CONCURRENT_REQUESTS_PER_DOMAIN = 1` (fallback 8), `DOWNLOAD_DELAY = 1` s (fallback 0), `DOWNLOAD_DELAY_JITTER = 0.5` (added in 2.19.0), `ROBOTSTXT_OBEY = True` (fallback False), DNS cache on (10,000 entries) and no memory limit by default — [Scrapy settings](https://docs.scrapy.org/en/latest/topics/settings.html)
- **Goodfetch prototype.** Its scheduler uses at most 2 simultaneous requests per domain; with a site answering in 20 ms, that caps one domain at 100 jobs/s — [admission-2026-10-08.md](/Users/jb/Dev/automan/rnd/lab/reports/admission-2026-10-08.md)
- **Crawlee autoscaling.** Per ADR 0040, Crawlee scales on memory, CPU, event-loop delay and API 429s, and by default uses about 22% of memory (0.9 x `availableMemoryRatio` 0.25) — [ADR 0040](/Users/jb/Dev/automan/docs/adr/0040-one-resource-governor-per-engine-sized-from-measured-load.md)

### Inferences
- **Wall time per domain.** At about 1 request/s per domain (Scrapy's polite defaults), a 1,000-page single-site crawl takes about 17 minutes however fast the client is. One vCPU spends only about 15 s of CPU on extraction for those 1,000 pages, so a cloud crawl API needs hundreds to thousands of domains in flight per vCPU to keep CPU busy. Its cost driver is then memory per open connection and per in-flight page, plus proxy bandwidth, not CPU.
- **Per-vCPU throughput with extraction included.** With rs-trafilatura at about 14.5 ms per page as the dominant CPU cost, one vCPU sustains roughly 60 to 70 pages/s of fetch plus main-content extraction (about 250,000 pages per vCPU-hour), ignoring TLS and decompression. With Scrapy's Python stack, the no-op ceiling is about 50 pages/s per process before any parsing.

### Gaps
- No credible, like-for-like, published benchmark of pages per second per vCPU across Spider, Colly, Scrapy and Crawlee on the same corpus and politeness settings was found.
- No figure for TLS handshake CPU per new connection or for decompression CPU on real page sizes.
- No published data on politeness-limited throughput achieved in practice by commercial crawl APIs (Firecrawl, Spider cloud).

## 4. Bandwidth per page: HTML-only versus full page load, compressed versus uncompressed, and proxy GB per 1,000 pages

### Takeaway
An HTTP-only fetch of the HTML document moves about 14 to 33 KB compressed at the median (about 78 KB uncompressed), or about 0.014 to 0.033 GB per 1,000 pages. A full cold-cache browser load moves about 2.2 to 2.9 MB at the median (p90 8 to 9 MB), or about 2.2 to 2.9 GB per 1,000 pages. Blocking images, fonts and video removes roughly half of that, leaving about 0.7 to 0.8 GB per 1,000 pages, mostly JavaScript. HTTP-first therefore cuts proxy bandwidth roughly 65 to 160 fold against full loads, and about 20 to 45 fold against blocked loads.

### Cited Findings
- **Web Almanac 2025 (July 2025 crawl).** Median page weight across all pages is 2,412 KB desktop and 2,164 KB mobile; percentiles 10/25/50/75/90 are 607/1,275/2,412/4,570/9,179 KB desktop and 516/1,127/2,164/4,119/8,337 KB mobile. Median home pages are 2,862 KB desktop and 2,559 KB mobile; median inner pages about 2 MB desktop and 1.8 MB mobile. Median requests: 77 desktop and 72 mobile (all pages; a figure caption gives 73 and 68), 80 and 75 for home pages. Video bytes on the median page rose 28% year over year, from 246 KB to 315 KB. The chapter does not state whether sizes are transfer or decoded bytes, except that its JavaScript data are compressed bytes — [Web Almanac 2025 Page Weight](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/page-weight)
- **Web Almanac 2025, home-page medians by type** (mobile / desktop): HTML 22 / 22 KB, CSS 77 / 82 KB, fonts 122 / 139 KB, JS 632 / 697 KB, images 911 / 1,058 KB; video 384 / 247 KB (all pages) — [Web Almanac 2025 Page Weight](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/page-weight)
- **Web Almanac 2025, compression.** Proper text compression was found on 71% of desktop and 72% of mobile home-page loads (inner pages 72% and 73%), so over 25% of pages still lack efficient compression — [Web Almanac 2025 Page Weight](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/page-weight)
- **Web Almanac 2024 (October 2024).** Median page weight 2,652 KB desktop and 2,311 KB mobile; median requests 71 and 66. Home-page medians: HTML 18 KB, CSS 78 / 73 KB, fonts 131 / 111 KB, JS 613 / 558 KB, images 1,054 / 900 KB (desktop / mobile) — [Web Almanac 2024 Page Weight](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2024/page-weight)
- **Web Almanac 2024 Markup chapter.** HTML document transfer size, compression included: median 33 kB desktop and 32 kB mobile (30 and 29 kB in 2023); mobile percentiles 6/13/32/71/147 kB. HTML compression: Brotli 37% mobile and 36% desktop, gzip 52% and 53%, none about 10.5% to 11%. The median page has 594 elements (mobile). This 32 to 33 kB median conflicts with the 18 to 22 KB HTML medians in the Page Weight chapters, which likely use different scopes (home pages versus all pages, document versus all HTML responses) — [Web Almanac 2024 Markup](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2024/markup)
- **Researcher measurement on the WCXB test split** (511 real pages from the lab's local corpus): raw HTML is 77.6 KiB uncompressed at the median (p10 34.2, p90 157.3, mean 89.7). Recompressed with gzip level 6 it is 13.5 KiB at the median (p10 6.9, p90 22.8), a median compression ratio of 5.7x (p10 4.0, p90 8.6). These pages are post-render snapshots, and it is not known whether inline scripts were kept as served — [computed from rnd/lab/corpora/wcxb/test](/Users/jb/Dev/automan/rnd/lab/corpora/wcxb/test)
- Browserbase bills proxy bandwidth by the MB, with a 1 MB minimum per session — [Browserbase plans](https://docs.browserbase.com/account/billing/plans.md)

### Inferences
Researcher arithmetic, decimal units (1 KB x 1,000 pages is about 1 MB):
- **HTTP-only, HTML document only.** 0.014 GB (WCXB gzip median), 0.018 to 0.022 GB (Almanac home-page HTML medians) or 0.033 GB (Almanac markup median) per 1,000 pages. When the server does not compress (about 10% of pages), use about 0.078 GB per 1,000 (WCXB uncompressed median). TLS handshakes and headers add an unquantified few KB per new connection; connection reuse on single-site crawls amortizes them.
- **Full browser load, cold cache.** About 2.2 to 2.9 GB per 1,000 pages at the median, 8.3 to 9.2 GB at p90. The mean is higher than the median because of the long tail.
- **Browser with images, fonts and media blocked.** About 0.73 to 0.80 GB per 1,000 pages (sum of the median HTML, CSS and JS). Medians do not add exactly, so this is an approximation. Images, fonts and video together are about 50 to 55% of the median page's bytes on the same sum-of-medians basis.
- **Same-site crawls with a warm cache.** They transfer less per page than cold-cache figures suggest, because shared JS and CSS are reused across a Session's pages. The Almanac numbers are first loads. Lazy-loaded or infinite-scroll content adds bytes beyond the initial load.
- **Proxied HTTP-first versus proxied browser.** Against HTML-only medians of 18 to 33 KB, the bandwidth ratio is roughly 65 to 160x with all resources loaded and about 20 to 45x with images, fonts and media blocked. It is higher against the WCXB gzip median of 13.8 KB.

### Gaps
- No primary source gives median uncompressed HTML size across the web. The Almanac chapters report transfer sizes, or leave the basis unstated.
- No measured, published dataset of bytes per page for browser loads with resource blocking, nor of warm-cache bytes per page across a same-site crawl.
- No measured TLS and HTTP overhead per request on real crawls.

### Takeaway

### Inferences
- **Sizing the browser tier.** For a cost model, the share of pages needing a browser should be a parameter, not a constant. The evidence suggests most pages' main text is in raw HTML (Almanac word counts; the lab's adaptive routing keeps the browser only where the HTTP sample disagrees). The minority that needs JS, together with anti-bot pages that need a real browser fingerprint, sizes the browser tier.
- **Anti-bot exposure.** About 27% of sites behind Cloudflare is an upper bound on Cloudflare exposure: challenges depend on each site's bot settings and on the client's IP and fingerprint. Headless Chromium on datacenter IPs passing only 2% on protected sites shows that, on those sites, the challenge rate depends far more on IP type and fingerprint than on page type.

### Gaps
- No primary measurement of the share of web pages whose content needs JavaScript rendering.
- No public share of escalations to a browser in a production crawl API (Firecrawl, Spider, Zyte).
- No published captcha or challenge encounter rate per request or per site for scraping traffic, from Cloudflare, DataDome, Akamai or academic work.
- No share of websites using DataDome, PerimeterX/HUMAN or Kasada bot management was found; W3Techs lists reverse proxies only.

### Inferences
- **Tokens per agent step.** Screenshot agents pay about 1,000 to 1,800 tokens per image at common web resolutions, re-sent with history unless pruned. DOM or accessibility-tree agents pay for serialized page state: about 3,000 tokens per step if the Notte run averaged about 10 steps (an assumption, since steps were not reported). With history accumulation, a 10 to 30-step task reaches tens of thousands of input tokens, which matches the about 31,000 measured for Browser-Use in 2025.
- **Agent-task time drives browser-hours.** An agent task holds a browser for about 1 to 11 minutes (68 s for the fastest 2025 agent; 268 to 678 s on 2026 hard evals; up to 44 minutes for Operator on hard tasks). At vendor sizing (about 0.3 to 0.8 GB, 0.16 to 0.4 vCPU per session), one task costs about 0.02 to 0.2 browser-hours, and up to about 0.7 for a 44-minute hard task. Model tokens usually dominate the cost of a task with frontier models, but not with the cheapest models ($0.02 to $0.07 per task in the Stagehand evals).
- **LLM extraction per 1,000 pages.** About 1.6M input tokens of main content at the median, versus about 20M if raw HTML were sent. Isolating main content before the model call is therefore about a 12x token saving at the median, before output tokens and schema prompts.
- **Replay.** A learned plan replays with zero or one model call per run (lab: 1 call per 5-run session plus 1 repair on drift). The agent's token cost then moves from every run to first runs and repairs, and the cloud cost becomes browser or HTTP time plus occasional repair tokens.

### Gaps
- No vendor publishes measured input and output tokens per step for DOM, accessibility-tree and screenshot modes on the same tasks.
- No WebArena step or cost figures were collected; OpenAI's CUA page returned 403, so Operator figures come only from the Online-Mind2Web paper.
- No published cache hit rates or measured savings for Stagehand caching, Browser Use skills or workflows, or Skyvern code generation; Skyvern's README makes no code-generation cost claim.
- Token counts per page used a character heuristic, since no tokenizer was installed; real counts vary by tokenizer and are higher for HTML.

## 7. Typical usage shapes: session durations and pages per session on hosted-browser platforms, Apify Actor run sizes, and the share of short runs

### Takeaway
No platform publishes the distribution of session durations, pages per session or run sizes. Only caps, defaults and billing granularity are public: Browserbase sessions are billed at least 1 minute, a Steel session defaults to 5 minutes (24 h maximum), a Cloudflare browser idles out after 60 s, and an Apify browser Actor needs at least 1 GB, with 1 vCPU per 4 GB. Agent tasks run about 1 to 11 minutes each.

### Cited Findings
- Browserbase plan limits (concurrency / maximum session duration / session creation rate): Free 3 / 15 min / 5 per minute; Developer 25 / 6 h / 25 per minute; Startup 100 / 6 h / 50 per minute; Scale 250+ / 6+ h / 150+ per minute. Browser time is billed by the minute and proxy by the MB, with a 1-minute and 1-MB minimum per session — [Browserbase plans](https://docs.browserbase.com/account/billing/plans.md)
- Browserbase answers 429 when a concurrency or creation limit is reached, and suggests keep-alive to reuse a session for short-lived work (via search summary, not fetched) — [Browserbase concurrency](https://docs.browserbase.com/optimizations/concurrency/overview.md)
- Steel: the default session timeout is 5 minutes, up to 24 hours depending on plan. `inactivityTimeout` is off by default, and the timeout of a live session cannot be changed — [Steel session lifecycle](https://docs.steel.dev/overview/sessions-api/session-lifecycle)
- Cloudflare Browser Run: a browser is killed after 60 s without a DevTools command, and `keep_alive` extends that to 10 minutes. Newest paid limits: 200 concurrent browsers per account and 3 new instances per second (older docs list 10 or 30) (via search summary, not fetched) — [Cloudflare Browser Run limits](https://developers.cloudflare.com/browser-run/limits/index.md)
- Kernel browsers can be paused for up to 72 hours and resumed from the same state — [Unikraft case study](https://unikraft.com/customer-stories/scaling-browser-infrastructure)
- Apify Actor memory is a power of 2 from 128 MB to 32,768 MB, with 1 CPU core per 4,096 MB and disk limited to twice the memory. A compute unit is 1,024 MB for 1 hour. Autoscaling only applies to runs that process multiple URLs for at least 30 seconds, and single-job Actors are advised to lower memory. Larger batches are recommended over many short runs, which repeat start-up. Worked example: an hourly 15-minute run at 4,096 MB uses about 1 CU per run — [Apify usage and resources](https://docs.apify.com/platform/actors/running/usage-and-resources)
- Hosted-browser lifecycle overhead for a single navigation (create, connect, goto, release) has a median of 0.87 to 1.67 s on the three fastest providers — [Steel benchmark](https://steel.dev/blog/remote-browser-benchmark)
- Agent task durations: 68 to 330 s on Online-Mind2Web in October 2025 ([Browser Use speed post](https://browser-use.com/posts/speed-matters)); 268 to 678 s on 2026 hard evals ([Stagehand evals](https://www.stagehand.dev/evals)); about 4.3 to 10 minutes on BU Bench ([BU Bench post](https://browser-use.com/posts/ai-browser-agent-benchmark)); up to 44 minutes for Operator on hard tasks ([Online-Mind2Web](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01382))
- Scale disclosures: Browser Use runs hundreds of thousands of agent tasks per day ([skills post](https://browser-use.com/posts/web-agents-that-actually-learn)) and has run 1,000-browser and 10,000-session stress tests ([Firecracker post](https://browser-use.com/posts/firecracker-browser-infra)). The Browserbase blog index shows no usage statistics — [Browserbase blog](https://www.browserbase.com/blog)
- Crawl-side defaults bound pages per browser: Crawlee retires a browser after 100 pages and allows at most 20 open pages per browser ([crawlee browser-pool.ts](https://github.com/apify/crawlee/blob/v3.18.2/packages/browser-pool/src/browser-pool.ts)); Goodfetch ADR 0040 also recycles after 100 pages ([ADR 0040](/Users/jb/Dev/automan/docs/adr/0040-one-resource-governor-per-engine-sized-from-measured-load.md))

### Inferences
- **Billing granularity matters for short sessions.** With 1-minute billing minimums and lifecycle overheads of about 1 to 2 s, very short sessions (one navigation, often under 10 s of real work) pay mostly for the minimum and for set-up. This favours keeping sessions alive across tasks, and pre-warmed pools or snapshots.
- **Two usage shapes.** Agent workloads are long and idle-heavy (minutes per task, mostly waiting on the model), so they are memory-bound per session-hour. Crawl workloads are page-dense (up to 100 pages per browser before recycling) and CPU-bound per page.
- **Apify's guidance implies a short-run overhead.** Its advice against many short runs, and autoscaling only after 30 s, means short runs carry a fixed start-up overhead. The size of that overhead and the share of such runs are not public.

### Gaps
- No public distribution (median, percentiles) of session duration or pages per session from Browserbase, Steel, Kernel, Hyperbrowser or Browser Use.
- No public statistics on Apify Actor run sizes (memory chosen, duration) or on the share of short runs on the Apify platform or Store.
- No public split of hosted-browser usage between agent and scraping workloads.
